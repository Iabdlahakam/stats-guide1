# Research Question Starter Pack

## Purpose

The **Research Question Starter Pack** is the first branded educational product for Clinical Research Compass. It should help a learner turn a broad clinical idea into a structured, answerable research question before investing time in protocol writing, data collection, or statistical analysis.

## Pack Contents

1. Branded cover page.
2. PICOT worksheet for intervention, exposure, and comparative effectiveness questions.
3. PEO worksheet for qualitative, exploratory, exposure, and experience-focused questions.
4. SPIDER worksheet for qualitative and mixed-methods questions.
5. Validation checklist for each framework.
6. Matching infographic post for each framework.
7. Light CRC watermark and branded footer on every page.

## Educational Post Model

Every framework post should be a complete educational explainer, not a short quote. Use this structure:

1. **Short clinical hook:** State a common problem in research planning.
2. **Definition:** Explain what the framework is and when to use it.
3. **Key components:** Define each letter clearly.
4. **Step-by-step use:** Show how to move from idea to question.
5. **Common mistakes:** Explain what weakens the question.
6. **Worked example:** Use a realistic clinical example.
7. **Practical template or checklist gift:** Offer the worksheet.
8. **Signature CTA:** “Comment COMPASS for the worksheet.”

## PICOT Framework

### When to Use PICOT

Use PICOT when the project asks a focused clinical question about an intervention, exposure, comparison, outcome, and timeframe. It is especially useful for clinical trials, cohort studies, retrospective chart reviews, and comparative effectiveness projects.

### Components

| Component | Meaning | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Population | Who is being studied? | Which patients, setting, disease, or eligibility criteria define the group? |
| Intervention / Exposure | What is being tested or observed? | What treatment, exposure, strategy, risk factor, or program is central to the question? |
| Comparison | What is it compared against? | Standard care, no exposure, another treatment, historical control, or another group? |
| Outcome | What will be measured? | What clinical, behavioral, utilization, quality, or safety outcome matters most? |
| Timeframe | Over what period? | When will the outcome be assessed? |

### Worked Example

**Clinical idea:** Does telemedicine follow-up help adults with type 2 diabetes improve glycemic control?

| PICOT Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Population | Adults with type 2 diabetes |
| Intervention | Telemedicine follow-up |
| Comparison | Standard in-person care |
| Outcome | Change in HbA1c |
| Timeframe | 6 months |

**Final PICOT question:** Among adults with type 2 diabetes, does telemedicine follow-up compared with standard in-person care improve HbA1c over 6 months?

### Common PICOT Mistakes

- Defining the population too broadly.
- Including multiple primary outcomes without prioritization.
- Choosing an outcome that is not measurable in the available data source.
- Forgetting the comparison group.
- Leaving the timeframe undefined.

## PEO Framework

### When to Use PEO

Use PEO when the question focuses on a population, exposure, and outcome without requiring a formal comparison group. It is useful for exploratory, qualitative, observational, public health, and experience-focused research questions.

### Components

| Component | Meaning | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Population | Who is affected? | Which patients, clinicians, learners, or communities are being studied? |
| Exposure | What experience, condition, or context matters? | What event, risk factor, environment, or care pathway is relevant? |
| Outcome | What result, perception, or experience is being explored? | What outcome, behavior, belief, barrier, or clinical result matters? |

### Example

**Question:** Among internal medicine residents, how does night-float rotation exposure influence perceived burnout and sleep quality?

### PEO Validation Checklist

- [ ] The population is clearly bounded.
- [ ] The exposure or context is specific.
- [ ] The outcome is observable, measurable, or explorable.
- [ ] The question does not force an unnecessary comparison group.
- [ ] The design is appropriate for observational or qualitative inquiry.

## SPIDER Framework

### When to Use SPIDER

Use SPIDER for qualitative and mixed-methods projects that need a structured way to define the sample, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, and research type.

### Components

| Component | Meaning | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Sample | Who will participate? | Which participants or records will be included? |
| Phenomenon of Interest | What experience or process is being studied? | What clinical, educational, or system issue is central? |
| Design | How will data be collected? | Interviews, focus groups, surveys, observations, chart review, or mixed methods? |
| Evaluation | What will be evaluated? | Experiences, barriers, perceptions, acceptability, feasibility, or outcomes? |
| Research Type | What type of study is this? | Qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods? |

### Example

**Question:** Among first-year residents, what barriers and facilitators influence completion of quality-improvement projects during inpatient rotations?

### SPIDER Validation Checklist

- [ ] The sample is specific enough to recruit or identify.
- [ ] The phenomenon of interest is not too broad.
- [ ] The design matches the kind of data needed.
- [ ] The evaluation target is clear.
- [ ] The research type is stated.
- [ ] The question can guide data collection and analysis.
